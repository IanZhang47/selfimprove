<a id="stages"></a>
## 1. Set a stage for one topic

Use these stages separately for each discipline or subfield. They are self-study milestones, not degree equivalents.

| Stage | What you work on | Evidence of progress |
|---|---|---|
| 0 — Locate | Main questions, scope, branches, applications, and vocabulary | Explain what the field studies and where its boundaries are |
| 1 — Foundations | A suitable introductory text or course, with its essential activities | Explain central ideas accurately and solve representative introductory tasks |
| 2 — Working competence | A coherent course, skills sequence, or supervised practice route, with prerequisites and applied work appropriate to the field | Complete unfamiliar problems, source analyses, designs, or projects with feedback |
| 3 — Specialist study | Advanced texts, competing models, reviews, primary research, or advanced practice | Assess assumptions and methods; locate disagreement; reproduce or critically examine a result |
| 4 — Maintain/integrate | Updates, correction, teaching, cross-field application, and continued practice | Retain competence, revise claims, and recognize when another specialist is needed |

You can study foundations in one field while working deeply in another. You do not need to survey all fields before progressing. Equally, reaching Stage 3 in one area does not remove the other areas from your lifetime plan.

For a field you have already studied, sample its learning outcomes and representative problems first. Retained knowledge may justify skipping chapters. A course title alone is insufficient to certify mastery, and an absent course does not prove ignorance.

<a id="prerequisites"></a>
## 2. Check the prerequisites

Maintain a foundation route that supports other subjects. Choose the appropriate entry level rather than restarting everything:

1. Reading, writing, explanation, argument analysis, and information verification.
2. Algebra, functions, graphs, measurement, units, and numerical estimation.
3. Logic and proof where the chosen discipline needs them.
4. Probability, statistical reasoning, study design, and causal reasoning.
5. Learning methods, research organization, and a small correction log.
6. Listening, communication, cooperation, practical planning, and everyday financial literacy.

The [starting library](reference/02-CHECKED-LIBRARY.md) includes resources for these areas. Some are references to consult, not books to finish before beginning another subject.

Examples of dependency routes:

- **Physics and engineering:** algebra/trigonometry → calculus and vectors → introductory physics/mechanics → differential equations, linear algebra, and numerical methods as the specialty requires → advanced engineering with laboratory/design work.
- **Biological and health science:** introductory biology and chemistry → relevant organic chemistry, biochemistry, physiology and statistics → specialist science or professional study. Clinical competence also requires supervised training.
- **Data and behavioral research:** probability/statistics → study design and measurement → regression and model criticism → causal methods or other specialist methods appropriate to the question.
- **History:** historical method and a chronological/geographical framework → regional scholarship and primary sources → competing explanations and a supported written account.
- **Philosophy:** argument reconstruction and introductory context → primary arguments with good commentary → comparative analysis. Formal logic is helpful for some branches, but it is not the entire philosophy curriculum.
- **Arts and languages:** introductory literacy → substantial contact with works, conversation, making, or performance → feedback and specialist study. Background reading cannot replace the activity itself.

These are route-design recommendations. Specific textbooks and departments differ in their sequencing. When opening a specialist route, compare current syllabi from more than one suitable institution and check that the prerequisites are present.

<a id="study-cycle"></a>
## 3. Use a repeatable study cycle

| Step | What to do | Keep |
|---|---|---|
| Choose | Define a small question, chapter range or skill; select a suitable resource. | One clear learning outcome |
| Learn | Read or watch carefully; connect ideas to examples and prior knowledge. | Short notes on the ideas you need |
| Retrieve and apply | Explain without looking, solve problems, write, build, perform or practise. | An attempt you can inspect |
| Check | Compare with solutions, credible sources or informed feedback. | Corrections and unresolved questions |
| Revisit | Return after an interval and try a new example. | Evidence of what you retained |
| Decide | Continue, repair a prerequisite, deepen the route or open another topic. | The next concrete task |

Use retrieval, spaced revisits and feedback when durable learning is the goal. The [National Academies' How People Learn II](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24783) and the [Learning Scientists' overview](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2016/8/18-1) are the existing sources for this guidance. Adapt the activity to the field; flashcards are one option, not the whole method.

Plan in adjustable six- to twelve-week windows. Estimate your workload after the first two sessions, including exercises and review. A small number of active topics can receive serious attention while every other field stays on your lifetime map.

<a id="social-practice"></a>
## 4. Practise social and practical skills

Treat social improvement as a set of learnable, contextual abilities. A social-science textbook explains evidence; a communication text organizes skills; a practitioner manual proposes strategies. Reading one does not certify the others.

| Ability | A small practice | What to assess |
|---|---|---|
| Listening | Summarize another person's point and invite a correction | Whether the summary represents their meaning |
| Clear explanation | Explain an idea to someone unfamiliar with it | What they understood; where the explanation was unclear |
| Conversation | Ask a relevant question and follow the answer | Attention, reciprocity, and respect for interest and boundaries |
| Assertiveness and boundaries | State a concrete request or limit respectfully | Clarity and respect, independently of whether the person agrees |
| Disagreement and conflict repair | Identify the issue, acknowledge your contribution where appropriate, and propose a next step | Accuracy, responsibility, and whether misunderstanding decreases |
| Cooperation | Agree on responsibilities and check progress | Reliability, coordination, and useful feedback |
| Negotiation | Rehearse a scenario; identify interests, alternatives, and possible agreements | Quality of preparation and mutual understanding; not merely getting your way |
| Relationship maintenance | Follow through, make contact appropriately, and repair missed commitments | Consistency and reciprocity over time |
| Intercultural communication | Ask about an unfamiliar expectation and compare perspectives | Avoiding stereotypes and adapting to the actual person/context |
| Speaking and presenting | Rehearse, record with appropriate consent, or request feedback | Structure, comprehensibility, pacing, and audience understanding |

Start with the library's psychology and interpersonal-communication resources, then add negotiation when relevant. Maintain a simple practice note: situation, behavior attempted, feedback, adjustment. A favorable interaction is useful feedback, not scientific proof that a technique works universally.

<a id="progress"></a>
## 5. Review what you can demonstrate

At the end of a study window, keep one piece of work: a solved problem set, explanation, source comparison, tested program, design, performance or demonstrated skill. Record the feedback, corrections and next task in [My Study Plan](01-MY-STUDY-PLAN.md).

For important claims, use the [source and claim record](reference/01-SOURCE-STANDARDS.md#claim-record). Recheck fast-changing information when applying it; update stable foundations when relevant corrections or gaps appear. Book completion and competence are separate records.

Once a year, browse the [Subject Map](03-SUBJECT-MAP.md) and [field checklist](reference/fields/00-INDEX.md), notice neglected areas and adjust your next routes. No fixed number of books or hours establishes mastery of every major.
