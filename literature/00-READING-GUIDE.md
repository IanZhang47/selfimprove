Select a work for a clear purpose: explore a form or tradition; examine language and narrative; understand a historical conversation; study a difficult moral question; develop expression; or experience the work itself. Include works outside established syllabi when they serve that purpose. Long survival is not required, so contemporary and less translated traditions stay in scope.

## Source status

**Candidate** entries still need their exact edition, translator, scope, and supporting sources checked before becoming a study commitment. **Source-checked** refers only to the identity, edition/version, scope, or access described in its linked record; it does not certify every assertion, interpretation, or aesthetic judgment. See the shared [source standards](../reference/01-SOURCE-STANDARDS.md).

Every catalog and language-edition entry is Candidate unless it explicitly says Source-checked or links to a record specifying a check. Grouped translation names are comparison sets. Author-only entries are discovery prompts, not finished assignments. Collections, complete works, series, and authors are not equivalent book counts; no claim that all traditions are covered is made.

## Reliable literary understanding

The question changes with the claim. A fictional character is not a real person, but claims about the wording of a novel, its publication, the society it depicts, or its psychological effects can be checked. A memoir can be both a literary work and testimony about real events. An appealing account of human behavior in a novel is not automatically a validated psychological finding.

| Question | Appropriate basis | What to record |
|---|---|---|
| What does this edition actually say? | A reliable text, identified edition, passage, and translation | Quotation or paraphrase with page, chapter, or line reference |
| What interpretation fits the work? | Close reading, pattern, context, and engagement with plausible alternatives | An argument and its evidence; distinguish it from authorial intent |
| What happened historically? | Source provenance, corroboration, and relevant historical scholarship | Supported facts, conflicting testimony, and uncertainty |
| What does an essay claim about society, health, or psychology? | Relevant disciplinary evidence as well as the essay’s argument | Separate observation, generalization, causal claim, and opinion |
| Is this aesthetically valuable to me? | Attentive experience, comparison, and an explicit account of the qualities valued | A reasoned preference, not an objective rank or scientific fact |
| Does reading improve my social conduct? | Practice with real people, consent, feedback, and reflection | What changed in a real interaction; a character analysis is not proof of interpersonal skill |

## A lifetime progression

The skills can progress even when the reading order is not chronological. You do not need to finish *Gilgamesh* before reading *Middlemarch*, or English literature before another tradition. Each stage can be repeated in a new language, genre, or region.

| Stage | Work to do | Evidence of progress | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0. Locate** | Read a complete short story, several poems, or a play; learn the relevant form and vocabulary | Describe what happens or what is said without confusing narrator, speaker, author, and character | Choose a form or tradition to revisit |
| **1. Foundations** | Study selected chapters of Kelly J. Mays’s *The Norton Introduction to Literature* ([S1](12-SOURCE-REGISTER.md#s1)), with its stories, poems, and plays | Make a defensible claim supported by two or three precise passages; acknowledge an alternative | Read a longer or more structurally difficult work |
| **2. Working competence** | Read a cluster of works; pair it with literary history, historical scholarship, a translation comparison, and contrasting scholarly interpretations | Write or discuss a sustained interpretation, cite its evidence, explain what context adds and what remains uncertain, and respond fairly to an objection | Define a focused research or creative project |
| **3. Specialist study** | Learn relevant language/history/theory; follow a critical edition’s bibliography into scholarly books and articles | Compare textual variants or critical arguments; produce a researched essay, annotated translation, performance analysis, or creative work with commentary | Obtain informed feedback and revise |
| **4. Maintain/integrate** | Reread, revisit scholarship, add underrepresented traditions and contemporary works | Correct an earlier claim, refine an interpretation, or transfer a reading skill to a new form | Begin another route at the appropriate level |

Norton is a source-checked teaching resource, not a universal canon or an infallible theory of interpretation. For historical method, the [R22: John Tosh’s *The Pursuit of History*](../reference/02-CHECKED-LIBRARY.md#r22), is a useful companion. Select theoretical approaches to answer actual questions; learn their assumptions rather than treating a school of criticism as a final answer.

## A reading cycle

Choose a primary work and one useful companion. Make an initial reading, record a question, reread a passage, consult the companion, and revise your account. A six- to twelve-week window can support a substantial cluster; shorter works may take a few sessions and large texts several windows. Set workload from your own reading and language experience rather than a universal pages-per-hour estimate.

For example, a narrative route could use three Chekhov stories, one story from another tradition, and selected Norton guidance on narration; a dramatic route could compare *Antigone*, *A Doll’s House*, and a staged production; a long-novel route could stay with *Middlemarch* or *红楼梦* for several windows. These are optional candidate routes, not prerequisites or rankings.

Use notes in a form that helps: digital, handwritten, audio, or discussion. For deliberate study, retain a short record of the edition, question, passage, interpretation, strongest alternative, and remaining factual questions. A commonplace book can preserve striking language, but copying by hand is not mandatory. Quote accurately and distinguish quotation from paraphrase.

Read aloud or listen when sound and performance matter; silent reading remains legitimate. Summaries can orient you or clarify a plot but cannot supply the same encounter with language, structure, or performance as the work itself. For factual knowledge in the other files, a summary also needs to be assessed for its omissions and evidence; it is not automatically sufficient.

Reread deliberately. Pause or change a work when the difficulty, timing, or edition obstructs the purpose. Diagnose whether the obstacle is language, missing context, form, or simple preference. No fixed page-count rule determines whether a work is worth continuing. Record what you learned before choosing the next step.

## Expression and social practice

Practice explaining a character’s possible motives while distinguishing textual evidence from speculation. Discuss a disagreement and summarize the other person’s interpretation to their satisfaction. Write a short reflection, argument, scene, or translation and invite specific feedback. Compare how a staged performance changes your interpretation.

These exercises can provide material for perspective-taking and communication; they do not justify claims that reading a particular author guarantees empathy, accurate judgments about strangers, or relationship competence. Use the communication and interpersonal routes in the [subject ladders](../03-SUBJECT-MAP.md) for research-based study, and practice listening, repair, boundaries, and cooperation in actual relationships.
