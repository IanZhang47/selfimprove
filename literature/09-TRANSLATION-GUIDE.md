# Choosing Translations and Reliable Texts

For each chosen edition, record the full title, author or attribution, editor, translator, publisher, year, ISBN if applicable, source text or recension, completeness, and what the notes cover. “Penguin Classics,” “Oxford,” or a respected Chinese publisher identifies a place to look, not a guarantee about every book in its catalog.

## Seven selection checks

1. **Identify the work and the version.** A retelling can introduce a narrative; a full translation supports a different task. For works transmitted orally or in multiple recensions, there may be no single uncontested “complete original.” Record what this edition translates and what it omits.
2. **Check language and method.** Look for the translator’s competence, the source edition, an introduction explaining choices, notes, and reviews by specialists. Direct translation is a useful criterion; it is not a guarantee against error. An intermediary translation or collaboration should be disclosed, not silently treated as the same process.
3. **Compare the actual work, not languages or publication dates in general.** Newer translations may use different scholarship or aims; they are not automatically more accurate. Chinese is not universally closer to Japanese, Russian, or French than English is. English is not universally closer either.
4. **Read the translator’s explanation of labels.** “Version,” “rendering,” and “after” may signal adaptation, but wording on a cover alone is not enough to determine the method or scope. Check the introduction and contents.
5. **Sample the same passage in two editions when the choice matters.** Compare omission, tone, names, syntax, poetry, explanatory additions, and notes. If you cannot read the source, the comparison shows differences, not which rendering is correct; consult a specialist discussion for that judgment.
6. **Use notes proportionately.** Historical and linguistic context can be necessary at any date. Read enough to understand the work, then return for deeper annotation. A first reading need not become continuous footnote study.
7. **Keep original-language study available.** Read the original when your skills support the task; use translations, glosses, recordings, and parallel texts while developing those skills. A supported translation can be more informative than misunderstanding an inaccessible original.

Three corrections are particularly important. FitzGerald studied Persian; describe his *Rubáiyát* as a free poetic adaptation rather than claiming he worked only from others’ cribs ([S2](12-SOURCE-REGISTER.md#s2)). Arthur Waley’s *Genji* is a translation whose choices can be compared with Royall Tyler’s, not categorically a non-translation ([S3](12-SOURCE-REGISTER.md#s3)). Satyamurti’s *Mahabharata* is a modern retelling, while van Buitenen’s translated portion is books 1–5, not the entire epic ([S4](12-SOURCE-REGISTER.md#s4)–[S5](12-SOURCE-REGISTER.md#s5)).

Nicholson, Jawid Mojaddedi, and Coleman Barks remain distinct Rumi routes to investigate; identify direct translation, revision, adaptation, and intermediary sources for the exact volume. Neither popularity nor an appealing style settles fidelity. The same standard applies to every translation candidate in this file.
